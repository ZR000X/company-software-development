

The principle here is the fundamentality of how important business process are to define, enforce, and improve. 

A business can be fundamentally modeled as a system of agenta solving problems that provide value to customers who pay for solutions. [Referenciable: Personal MBA]. The use of the word 'agent' here pays worthy adue to the fact that, in the Age of AI, it is possible to think of agents not necessarily as people but any entity that is capable of agency, that is, taking action when prompted with a problem. 

Furthering this train of thought, the processes within a business can be fundamentally modeled as systems of operations, where each operation itself can be modeled as a transaction with a hypothetical database. Thinking in terms of data, we see the business model itself as a cluster of agentic mental models, whether or not the agents are human or AI. Each mental model can then be modeled as a data model, relational or otherwise, to arrive at the final conclusion: when done deliberately and with proper modeling skills, a business's model and operations can be understood as a data model equipped with operations that are essentially operating with that data model. Processes are then perhaps idealized as procedures that modify the business data. Note: I am not supposing a literal database, but from the lens of someone familiar with data modeling, database design, and understanding how business capabilities need to map to software application features and the databases behind them, I can see how a business functions as a cluster of Excels that eventually must become more matured business applications. Even small to medium businesses need to understand that, in order to grow beyond having a few smart people who know how to solve problems on the fly, you must have processes that supply talent, track work and time, account for costs against revenue, well enough to reliable replicate past successes and lessons learned in the future even as people come and go.

At the core, the business is a financial entity. It must spend money, borrow money when needed, and make money eventually to cover the costs of running the business, eventually also making enough to cover the risk and time it took to make the money. The basic operation happening here in most capitalist economies is investors agreeing that their investment is worth the risk considering the potential profit they could make, while in other cases governments can also align their own considerations to what is worth investing the upfront cost for the potential profit. So unless processes are installed that serve those ends, they are not worth it. A process that is defined and hopes to be worth it, would need to admit what it costs and explain what it provides. Like a miniature business in itself, a process acts like a tiny machine that costs money to operate and must produce a profit. While it may not make sense to literally track the happenings of a process down to being able to report on its specific profitability, as the administrative costs of doing so need to be weighed, it nevertheless makes sense to engineer business processes with the financial impact in mind. 

A process costs capacity, that it, it requires certain agents with certain skills to function. It also often depends on the presence of and alignment with other processes, so one must now think about integration between processes, and this itself becomes a meta-process. Now the mentioning of a "meta-process" may seem overkill, but it means nothing more than having meetings between agents responsible for the management of their respective processes and ensuring they are able to reconcile between themselves. For example, a scrum master runs a process of coordinate amongst a team of developers to build a piece of software and then to fix any bugs or missed requirements. They depend on solution architects having a process of defining the initial work that must be done, and they also depend on a QA team logging any issues they pick up, bugs in software or acceptance criteria not being met. But where does the acceptance criteria come from? A process of capturing the customer's requirements and documenting them in a format approved by the customer that was fed into the initial architecture and solution design. The project manager's process then relies on the scrum master's reports to understand what the team is spending their time on in order to report to senior management on the progress of the project, so that financial projections and strategic decisions can be made. At every level of the business, processes are required. They must work together, and although they may not all be written down, they are happening in the minds of the agents running the business. The necessity to write them down comes when the business grows. When a lot of work comes into the business, there is a need to spend more money to meet the demand. The risk increases that the cost is underestimated, and the pricing is not feasible. For small projects with enough smart people, that together have the skills and intellect required to navigate the problems and improvise their solutions, the risk is low. But with large projects, scope management, timeline management, stakeholder management, capacity management, all become more necessary. The mindset of the business must shift, from focusing entirely on providing customer value to now also taking defensive measures into account. As the workload increases, so does the risk of burnout, of losing track of operations, finding yourself in a position where you have overcommitted and underpriced. Now the business loses money. In a startup culture, feasibility hardly factors. As the business matures and seeks to stabilize, reliable financial outcomes matter. And process it the fundamental tool with which to create repeatable, measurable operations that do not rely on the direct and consistent application of specific, limited, talented people. 

But then what is a process? How does one define it? Where do you store the definition? How do you enforce it? How do you measure its impact? How do you measure its cost? How do you socialize it into the business? How do you ensure it stays relevant and adaptable in an environment to which it is deployed?

The answers to these questions are business architecture and business engineering problems, and I believe they are best modeled as software architecture and software engineering problems. Now, that is not to say you literally need specific software behind any of your business problems, but I am making the case that to even talk about a process, you need a model. To then even talk about operating with this process, you will default to talking about the data involved. You will talk about metrics. You will talk about actions that change the state of some quantifiable things. This will inform your data model. The actions within the process can be imagined as users of some hypothetical software, with a central database behind it. Roles and permissions. All of the basic concepts within software design can be applied to business design. IT is at the heart of the business, if only they understood the business itself. There should not be such a gap between business and IT, but often the skillsets are different so people do not become experts at both. But there are many people who do master both business and IT at technical levels. Sadly, in reality, they tend to only operate the business instead of leading it. Leadership and political influence within an organisation is beyond the scope of this article, as well as its relation to technical strength. The point made here is only that true, technical strength, ought to be understood as having two fundamental components: business and IT, and strong people should not be comfortable only having one of these components.
