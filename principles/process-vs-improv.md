# 1 Process versus improvisation: know the terrain

Choosing how a team works is not a one-size-fits-all decision. The same smart people can thrive in one context and stall in another because workflow is environmental. Process design is a maturity move: you encode what you have learned so others can repeat it. That is a different activity from innovation in the sense of deliberately challenging established practice to find a better method. Both matter; they are not the same job.

This article is about workflow discovery: diagnosing the terrain before you arm the team. Get it wrong and you either send improvisers into a minefield of handoffs and unwritten rules, or you smother exploration with ceremony that no longer buys safety.

Key takeaways

- Mature process is encodement and teachability, not a substitute for innovation (questioning how work should be done).
- Terrain matters: mature organizations reward process literacy and preparation, not raw talent alone.
- Improvisation fits exploration-heavy contexts; explicit process fits coordination-heavy contexts—dominant risk is the guide.
- Before changing ways of working, ask what fails if coordination breaks, how reversible the choice is, and what the environment already expects.

# 2 Innovation versus encodement

Innovation, in the sense used here, is what happens when capable people challenge what is taken for granted and search for a new way to solve a hard problem. It produces insight, prototypes, and sometimes a breakthrough in *how* work should flow.

Process maturity is what happens after something works often enough that you want it to survive turnover, scale, and time. You name steps, clarify handoffs, train newcomers, and make expectations inspectable. That is encodement: turning tacit skill into something the organization can run without the original inventors in the room.

Confusing the two creates predictable pathologies. Teams asked to “innovate the process” every quarter never stabilize enough to measure improvement. Teams that freeze the first workflow they try starve legitimate experimentation. Healthy organizations run both modes in different places at different times, on purpose.

# 3 The administrative and social terrain

Large or long-lived organizations are not neutral ground. They accumulate implicit rules, approval paths, budget rhythms, reputation with stakeholders, and veterans who know how work really gets done. A newcomer with a strong intellect but no structure, training, or process literacy still hits the same obstacles; the environment does not grade on curiosity alone.

In that terrain, process and explicit structure behave like equipment: they reduce surprise, align expectations, and make it possible to coordinate without constant renegotiation. Entering that space unprepared—treating every gate as an annoyance to bypass—is how capable people become liabilities to themselves and to the teams depending on them.

The old strategic advice still applies: success depends on knowing the ground you fight on, not only on the quality of your side’s talent.[^1]

Workflow discovery means doing that reconnaissance before you standardize or dismantle a way of working: what are the real handoffs, who holds veto power, what fails loudly when someone skips a step?

# 4 When improvisation wins, when process wins

Both modes need smart people. The difference is which failure mode dominates if you guess wrong.

## 4.1 When improvisation tends to fit

Improvisation and light structure tend to fit when the problem is ill-defined, the cost of trying things is low and reversible, the team is small and mutually trusted, and the prize is learning or option value more than predictable throughput. Early product discovery, greenfield architecture spikes, and small incident response often look like this. Startup culture is not magic; it is appropriate when exploration is the main economic bet.

## 4.2 When process tends to fit

Explicit process tends to fit when many people must align, mistakes are expensive (safety, money, regulatory exposure), work crosses team boundaries, or people rotate often enough that tacit knowledge is not a reliable bus factor. Scale, compliance, and operational reliability push here. Process is not bureaucracy for its own sake; it is insurance against coordination failure.

The failure mode of excess structure is slow learning and demoralizing ceremony. The failure mode of excess improvisation is incidents, rework, and political debt. The job is to bias toward the failure you can afford least.

# 5 Practical heuristics

Before mandating a framework or declaring process “toxic,” ask:

- If two teams misunderstand a handoff tomorrow, what breaks first—revenue, safety, reputation, or merely someone’s calendar?
- How **reversible** is this way of working if we discover in six weeks that it was wrong?
- Does the environment already **expect** evidence (tickets, approvals, audit trails)? Fighting that without replacing it is not liberation; it is friction.
- Are we optimizing for **throughput of known work** or **rate of learning**? Name which one is primary for *this* initiative.

For why mature organizations turn process into something that looks like data, governance, and architecture, see the companion note [Business as process—and process as data](./business-as-process-and-process-as-data.md).

This piece does not cover people dynamics in depth—politics, career incentives, and informal power shape workflows too, and deserve their own reading. Here the focus stays on choosing a fit between improvisation and encodement given the terrain.
