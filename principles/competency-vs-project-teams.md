# 1 Competency lines and project delivery

Most organizations need both a stable home for professional practice and a cross-cutting mechanism to ship value on time. Problems start when those two structures are treated as interchangeable, or when one is asked to do the other’s job. Competency-based teams—functional lines, chapters, practice groups, or whatever name you use—are the natural owner of how work is done to a professional standard: review, quality, skills, and honest capacity. Project-based, product-based, or initiative-based structures are the natural owner of what gets built next, for whom, and under which dates—without becoming a substitute for the competency line’s obligations.

Project management and coordination remain essential. The pitfall is over-reliance on delivery roles to define good engineering, absorb peer review, or pretend that schedule pressure is the same as quality management.

Key takeaways

- The competency line answers: how good, how skilled, how sustainable, and how we improve the craft.
- The delivery line answers: what ships when, how priorities stack, and how customer-facing commitments are managed.
- Peer review, quality bars, and capacity truth for the discipline belong to the competency system; process outputs should flow through that system, not only through a project plan.
- Project and program managers coordinate and surface risk; they should not by default own the definition of professional quality or replace line leadership on people and skills.
- Quality and delivery pull in different directions; incentives must protect both, not only velocity.
- Mature organizations usually run a matrix: clarity of accountability matters more than the label on the org chart.[^1]

# 2 Two questions, two homes

Competency management is the foundation for people, skills, talent, and the long arc of capability in a discipline. It is where hiring bars, career growth, mentoring, and technical standards live. If you dismantle or hollow out that home, you still get work assigned by projects—but you lose a coherent place that says what “good” means and who is responsible for raising the bar.

Project and product delivery manage the value pipeline as seen by the customer: sequencing, dependencies, milestones, and communication with stakeholders who care about dates and scope. That work is real and hard. It is simply not the same job as sustaining engineering excellence across projects.

When these questions are conflated—when the project plan is the only place peer review happens, or when the competency lead has no say in capacity—you get brittle delivery and eroding craft. When they are separated cleanly, each side can negotiate tradeoffs in the open instead of smuggling them through status reports.

# 3 What the competency system carries

The competency system carries standards and how they evolve: coding or design guidelines, review expectations, definitions of done that reflect professional judgment, and escalation paths when quality is at risk. Peer review is a competency practice first; embedding it in a delivery workflow is fine, but accountability for the rigor and teaching in that review sits with the line.

Capacity management for the discipline also belongs here in substance: how many people can run at what sustainable load, what skills are missing for the work in flight, and when saying no is the correct answer. Delivery can aggregate asks and negotiate priorities; the line should own whether the ask is feasible without burning people out or cutting corners that will cost more later.

Talent and skills plans—who gets stretched, who gets trained, what the next generation of leads looks like—are competency outcomes. For how process and data eventually reflect capacity and operations at scale, see the companion note [Business as process—and process as data](./business-as-process-and-process-as-data.md).

# 4 What project and product delivery carries

Delivery carries prioritization across competing initiatives, alignment with customer or portfolio commitments, dependency mapping, and the politics of dates. It surfaces schedule risk, clarifies scope, and helps the organization decide when to cut scope versus move time. It is customer-facing in the sense that it translates chaos into a plan others can rely on.

What delivery should not silently absorb is the definition of professional quality, the authority to skip peer review because of a deadline, or sole ownership of whether the team is over capacity. Those decisions need a competency voice with equal weight. Orchestration without a strong line becomes optimism on a Gantt chart.

# 5 Incentives and the quality–delivery tradeoff

Quality and delivery are often opposed in the short term: thoroughness takes time; dates create pressure. Healthy organizations treat that tension as a negotiation between two legitimate systems, not as a mandate for the loudest sponsor.

When incentives reward only velocity or forecast accuracy, quality and learning lose by default. When incentives reward only internal purity and ignore delivery, the business loses trust. Project management metrics are poor proxies for engineering excellence; schedule green does not prove design or code health. Equating the two invites gaming and burnout. Long-standing quality thinking warns against managing people by numerical targets alone when those targets do not reflect real knowledge of the work.[^2]

# 6 Pitfalls and heuristics

Common pitfalls: the project manager named “quality owner” without a matching competency lead; review skipped under deadline with no line escalation; capacity treated as infinite until attrition proves otherwise; “just deliver” cultures that never fund debt reduction or training.

Quick tests for your design:

- If peer review were removed from the project plan tomorrow, would the competency line still require and resource it?
- Who can say no to a date on quality or capacity grounds, and do they have the same airtime as delivery leadership?
- Are people rewarded for developing others and raising standards, not only for shipping their slice?

This note does not endorse a specific scaling framework (SAFe, Spotify-style chapters and squads, and so on) and does not replace a full RACI or governance design. It states a principle: keep competency accountability for craft, people, and quality distinct from delivery accountability for commitments and the value pipeline, then connect them deliberately—usually in a matrix—rather than collapsing them into one overloaded role.
